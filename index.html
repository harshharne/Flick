Toss. Withdrawal. Instructions.

When a craving hits, the instructions you need are the ones you are least able to follow. So write them while you are calm, and toss for one when it hits.

Red pen is where I push back on it.

1Three words, split before they meet

Each word has more than one honest reading in the context of smoking. Before choosing, I laid them all out. The ones I kept are in ink; the ones I dropped are struck through, with the reason.

Toss verb

  • Chance. In a craving, the smoker bargains with themselves: just one. A toss hands the decision to something outside the bargaining.
  • Gesture. Every cigarette ends with a flick of the butt. The hands need an ending.
  • Tossing and turning. Insomnia is a real withdrawal symptom. Kept as a secondary thread.
  • Tossing the pack. Dropped: the single heroic quit is a myth. Most people take several attempts.

Withdrawal noun

  • Pharmacological. Symptoms peak within the first week and last two to four weeks (Hughes, 2007).
  • The break as withdrawal. The smoke break is a socially accepted way to step away. Quitting takes that permission too.
  • Social. Quitters lose the smokers' circle.
  • Money withdrawn. Dropped: "money saved" framing is already in every quit app.

Instructions noun

  • Imposed instructions. Dropped as a direction, kept as evidence: warnings already cover 85% of every pack in India.
  • Self-written instructions. If-then plans written in advance help people quit.
  • The ritual as instructions. Smoking is an instruction set the body runs on its own. Quitting means rewriting it.

Design a ritual object for the first weeks of quitting that carries the instructions a person wrote when calm into the minutes of a craving, and hands the decision back to them as the craving fades.

"Solve smoking" was too big to be honest about. Design can't touch nicotine pharmacology. It can touch the hands, the breath, the pause and the permission around it.

2The evidence, weighed rather than quoted

I didn't have time for fieldwork, so the research is literature. That makes it more important to say how much weight each finding can carry.

Well supported

A cigarette is more than nicotine

In controlled studies, smoke with the nicotine removed still reduced craving and felt more satisfying than not smoking, while nicotine delivered by IV reduced craving but brought no reported satisfaction (Rose et al., 2000).

But a review of sensorimotor substitutes found they may boost nicotine replacement therapy and are unlikely to work alone (Przulj et al., 2012). So this design complements nicotine replacement. It doesn't replace it.

Supported in the lab

Breathing helps, but people stop doing it

Controlled deep breathing reduced craving and irritability in dependent smokers (McClernon et al., 2004). Yogic breathing reduced all craving measures in the lab, but people barely practised it over the next day, and by 24 hours the effect was gone (Shahab et al., 2013).

This is the finding the project is built on. The technique isn't missing. Something to carry it into the moment is.

Supported, with wide variation

Instructions you write yourself work better

A meta-analysis of if-then plans ("if X happens, I will do Y") found quit rates of 10.7% with them against 4.9% without (McWilliams et al., 2019).

The studies differ a lot in what they count as an if-then plan. I treat this as direction, not proof.

Well supported

Knowing isn't the problem

Since 2016, pictorial warnings in India cover 85% of every pack and carry a quitline number. In GATS-2 (2016–17), 92.4% of adults believed smoking causes serious illness, yet 10.7% of adults still smoked.

So another warning, app or fact sheet is the wrong move. Imposed instructions have done what they can.

Weakly supported

Timing is everything, and nobody presses the button

Support that people trigger themselves at the right moment is seldom used, and automatically triggered support is probably not accurate enough yet to catch risky moments (Naughton, 2017). Individual cravings are commonly described as peaking within a few minutes.

The few-minutes figure comes from clinical guidance, not a trial I could read. And my answer to "nobody presses the button", an object already in the hand, is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Well supported

A substitute can keep the habit alive

Over 11 days of smoking nicotine-free cigarettes, the craving relief from smoking persisted, and the conditioned pull of the ritual faded only slowly (Donny et al., 2007).

Anything I design risks becoming the new cigarette. The object has to be designed to fade itself.

3The gap

The technique exists. The carrier doesn't.

Breathing, waiting out the urge, if-then plans: each works when people do it. What fails is the hand-off between the calm person who knows what to do and the craving person who can't concentrate. Poor concentration is itself a withdrawal symptom, so the moment instructions matter most is the moment they are hardest to follow.

There's a second gap. In India the chai-sutta break is a permission slip: a sanctioned reason to step out and stand with people. Quitting removes the cigarette and the permission together. I call this the permission gap.

The permission gap is my reading of the smoke break, not a measured finding. Fieldwork would test it first.

4The person and the moment

Rohan, 27, Bengaluru

Backend engineer. Eight cigarettes a day for six years, four of them on chai breaks with the same three colleagues. He quit on Saturday. It is now Tuesday, day three, the point where withdrawal is usually at its worst.

4:10 pm. Standup has just ended. Ankit leans over his monitor and says "chai?" Rohan's hand is already reaching for his pocket.

Rohan is invented from the research, not interviewed. Real testing would replace him with real people, and should include bidi smokers, who are the majority of Indian smokers and whom this concept serves least.

5The concept, and how it changed

First version: the coin of fate

A coin you flip when a craving hits. Heads, you smoke. Tails, you don't. It takes the toss literally and stops the bargaining by outsourcing the decision.

Killed. Half the time it tells someone in withdrawal to relapse. It treats a health decision as a joke, and it uses the same unpredictable-reward mechanism that makes gambling compelling. Its one good idea: a physical toss interrupts the bargaining.

Second version: Flick

A small die you carry in the pocket where the pack used to be. Every face is an instruction you wrote yourself, while calm, on the day you quit. When a craving hits, you toss it and do what it says for the next few minutes.

  1. Write. On quit day, fill each face with an instruction, from a starter set or your own. Faces use pictograms first and words second.
  2. Toss. When the craving hits, toss it. The gesture replaces the flick.
  3. Follow. Do what lands. Each instruction fills the few minutes a craving lasts.
  4. Retire. As withdrawal fades over the weeks, faces retire and turn blank. A blank face means your call.
"Retire" is the answer to the substitute problem. By week four, most faces are blank, so the die gradually hands the decision back to the person. If it never empties, it has become the new habit.

7Day three, 4:10 pm

  1. 4:10

    Ankit says "chai?" Rohan's hand goes to his pocket and finds the die where the pack used to be.

  2. 4:10

    He goes with Ankit anyway; the break isn't the problem, the cigarette is. At the tapri, he tosses. It lands on breathe, a face he drew himself on Saturday.

  3. 4:11

    In for four counts, out for six. Five rounds, the same rhythm as a long drag, while the others smoke and talk.

  4. 4:14

    The urge is still there but smaller. He drinks his chai with both hands.

  5. Day 19

    The water and call faces have retired. Two faces are now blank. When one lands, he decides for himself. Mostly, he doesn't smoke.

The scenario keeps the break and removes the cigarette. That only works if Rohan's colleagues don't push. A social version, where the circle knows about the die, is worth exploring.

8What would prove me wrong

If I had four weeks and twenty people quitting with nicotine replacement, I'd test these. Each one could kill the concept.

  • A plain timer works just as well. Compare craving ratings before and after a toss with a plain four-minute timer. If they match, the die adds nothing.
  • People don't toss. Count tosses against cravings logged. If the die stays in the pocket, "already in the hand" was wrong.
  • People don't write their own faces. If most keep the starter set, the self-written argument fails.
  • It never empties. If people still toss daily at week six, Flick has become the new cigarette.
I'd measure outcomes as "craving got through" and "urge reduced in the moment", never "helped people quit". That claim needs a proper trial.
